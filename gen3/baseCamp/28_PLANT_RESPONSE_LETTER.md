## 1. The Vegetation Consists of Native Michigan Species

The plants cited as "too tall" are native Michigan species, not noxious
weeds. Specifically:

[LIST SPECIES WITH SCIENTIFIC NAMES — from data collection template]

These species are native to Michigan as documented by the USDA PLANTS
Database (plants.usda.gov), Michigan Flora Online (michiganflora.net),
and the Michigan Natural Features Inventory (mnfi.anr.msu.edu).

Under the Michigan Noxious Weed Act (MCL 247.61-71), "noxious weeds"
are specifically defined as invasive species harmful to agriculture or
public health. Native Michigan plants do not meet this definition.

## 2. Native Plants Serve Critical Ecological Functions

The cited species provide:

- **Pollinator habitat** for native bees, butterflies, and other
  beneficial insects, consistent with the Michigan Pollinator Protection
  Plan (2019) published by the Michigan Department of Agriculture and
  Rural Development (MDARD)
- **Stormwater management** through deep root systems that reduce
  runoff, consistent with Michigan's MS4 stormwater requirements
- **Soil health** through microbial ecosystem support in the
  rhizosphere (root zone)

Removal of these species would degrade the ecological function of the
easement without any corresponding public benefit.

## 3. The Easement Does Not Grant Aesthetic Control

A [utility/right-of-way] easement grants the easement holder the right
to access and maintain [utility infrastructure / public passage]. It
does not grant the right to dictate the type, species, or aesthetic
appearance of vegetation on the property, provided that vegetation does
not physically interfere with the easement's designated purpose.

I request that you identify, in writing, the specific interference
these plants cause to the easement's designated function. If no
interference exists, the citation exceeds the scope of the easement.

## 4. Selective Enforcement

I have documented vegetation on neighboring easements within 200 feet
of my property that meets or exceeds the height of the cited plants,
including non-native invasive species that ARE harmful to Michigan
ecosystems. [See attached photographs.] These properties have not
received citations.

Selective enforcement of vegetation standards based on aesthetic
preference rather than ecological or public safety criteria raises
Equal Protection concerns under the Fourteenth Amendment and Article I,
Section 2 of the Michigan Constitution.

## 5. Vagueness

If the applicable ordinance uses subjective standards such as "too tall,"
"unsightly," or "weeds" without defining specific, measurable thresholds,
the ordinance may be unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process
Clause. I request that you provide the specific measurable standard
(in inches or centimeters) being applied and the legal authority for
that standard.

## 6. Request

I respectfully request:

1. **Withdrawal of the citation** on the grounds that native Michigan
   plants are not noxious weeds and do not interfere with easement
   function.

2. **Written identification** of the specific easement interference,
   if any, that these plants cause.

3. **Written identification** of the specific measurable height
   standard being enforced, with statutory or ordinance citation.

4. **Written explanation** of why non-native and invasive species on
   neighboring easements are not being cited under the same standard.

If this citation is not withdrawn, I intend to pursue an administrative
appeal and, if necessary, file a declaratory judgment action in Ingham
County Circuit Court seeking a judicial determination that native
Michigan plants are not subject to aesthetic enforcement on utility
easements.

published computational research in soil microbiology, precision
agriculture, and microbial ecosystem dynamics. I am familiar with
Michigan's native plant ecology and the ecological functions these
species provide. I am prepared to present expert testimony on the
ecological value of native plantings if this matter proceeds to
hearing or litigation.
