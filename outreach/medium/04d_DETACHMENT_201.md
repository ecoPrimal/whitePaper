## Detachment 201

On June 13, 2025, the Army Chief of Staff administered the Oath of Office
to four new Lieutenant Colonels.

Lieutenant Colonel is a field-grade rank. O-5. In a regular career path, it
takes approximately 16-18 years of service to reach. You commission as a
Second Lieutenant, you serve as a platoon leader, you command a company,
you serve on staff, you go to intermediate-level education, you command a
battalion if you're exceptional. The rank represents nearly two decades of
institutional knowledge, leadership under fire, and demonstrated competence
within the military system.

The four new Lieutenant Colonels had no military background. Zero. None of
them had served. None of them had gone through the process. None of them
had qualified at anything the military measures.

They received a two-week accelerated training course. Some of it online.
The rest at Fort Benning. It is unclear whether they had to meet fitness
or marksmanship standards.

Two weeks. To reach a rank that takes eighteen years.

The four:

- **Shyam Sankar** — Chief Technology Officer, Palantir Technologies.
  Sold $367.9 million in Palantir stock in 2024 alone.
- **Andrew Bosworth** — Chief Technology Officer, Meta. Just before his
  commissioning, Meta announced a defense partnership with Anduril for
  military AI and augmented reality contracts.
- **Kevin Weil** — Chief Product Officer, OpenAI. Within days of the
  ceremony, OpenAI announced a $200 million defense contract.
- **Bob McGrew** — Former Chief Research Officer, OpenAI. Adviser at
  Thinking Machines Lab.

They named the unit "Detachment 201" — a reference to HTTP status code
201: "Created." A tech industry in-joke, worn as a unit designation.

## The UCMJ and the Blue Book

The Uniform Code of Military Justice exists for a reason. It is the legal
framework that binds every servicemember to a standard of conduct. It
applies equally — in theory — from the newest Private to the most senior
General. It covers conflicts of interest, conduct unbecoming, and the
fundamental principle that military service is *service*, not a networking
opportunity.

The Blue Book — the Army's foundational leadership doctrine — establishes
that rank carries responsibility proportional to its authority. A
Lieutenant Colonel is responsible for the welfare and mission readiness of
a battalion-sized element, typically 300-1,000 soldiers. The rank is not a
title. It is a trust.

When four tech executives are commissioned as Lieutenant Colonels with no
service, no qualification, no institutional knowledge, and no recusal from
the business dealings that their companies conduct with the Department of
Defense — the trust is not being extended. It is being sold.

Army officials insist "they're not making acquisition decisions." The
companies involved secured over a billion dollars in contracts within days.
Palantir alone secured $759 million from the Army for AI development.

The system says: there are firewalls. The system also says: there is no
formal recusal requirement. The system says both things simultaneously,
and both are considered adequate.

## Who Shows Up

Here is what military service is supposed to look like:

You show up. You go through the process. You qualify or you don't.
The standards do not care about your net worth. The drill sergeant does
not care about your stock portfolio. The physical fitness test does not
grade on a wealth curve. The marksmanship qualification does not accept
stock options in lieu of a passing score.

The process is the point. It is the mechanism by which civilian identity
is transformed into military identity. It is how trust is established
within the chain of command — not through credentials or reputation, but
through shared experience and demonstrated competence.

An E-4 who went through basic, qualified expert on their weapon, deployed,
and carried the weight has more institutional legitimacy than an O-5 who
showed up for two weeks and got a ceremony.

This is not gatekeeping. This is the structural integrity of the
institution. When you bypass the process for people with money and
influence, you are not "bringing talent into the military." You are telling
every person who went through the process that the process was optional
for those who could afford to skip it.

## The Oath

The Oath of Office that those four executives took includes the words:
"I will support and defend the Constitution of the United States against
all enemies, foreign and domestic."

The Constitution they swore to defend includes the principle that the
military is subordinate to civilian authority — not that civilian
corporations should colonize the military for market access.

The UCMJ they are now subject to includes Article 134 — the general
article — which covers conduct "of a nature to bring discredit upon
the armed forces." A reasonable person could argue that commissioning
billionaires who won't recuse themselves from billion-dollar contracts
meets that standard.

But reasonable people are not making these decisions. The decisions are
being made by the same institutional architecture that writes manifestos
about national purpose while the people who actually served sleep
outside.
